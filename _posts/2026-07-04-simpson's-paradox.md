## What Is Simpson's Paradox?

Simpson's Paradox is a statistical phenomenon in which a trend appearing across separate groups disappears—or reverses—when those groups are combined.

Imagine comparing two strategies and discovering that **Strategy A performs better than Strategy B in every individual group**. Naturally, you would expect Strategy A to perform better overall.

Surprisingly, after combining the groups, the opposite can happen: **Strategy B appears to outperform Strategy A**.

This reversal does not mean the data is wrong. It usually happens because the groups differ in size or composition. When the underlying mix changes, the overall average can tell a very different story from the averages within each group.

## A Real-World Example: Visa CEDP

### What Is Visa CEDP?

Visa's **Commercial Enhanced Data Program (CEDP)** changed how B2B and business-card transactions qualify for discounted interchange rates.

The program replaces the legacy Level 2 and Level 3 programs with a centralized framework called **Product 3**.

### Timeline of Key Changes

The program's multi-phase rollout spans early 2025 through early 2026. It introduces penalties for missing or low-quality transaction data.

| Effective date | Change | Business impact |
|---|---|---|
| **April 2025** | Program launch and participation fee | Visa introduced a 5-basis-point (0.05%) fee on commercial transactions containing enhanced data. |
| **October 2025** | Data-quality enforcement | Visa replaced the old Level 3 system with the CEDP Product 3 framework and introduced stricter merchant-verification requirements. |
| **January 2026** | Small-business card adjustments | Visa implemented significant fee increases affecting Small Business Cards. |
| **April 2026** | Legacy Level 2 sunset | Visa retired the legacy Level 2 program. Product 3 compliance became the sole route to B2B discounts. |

## Why Simpson's Paradox Matters Here

When analyzing CEDP outcomes, an overall performance metric may hide important differences between merchant segments, card types, or transaction categories.

A program might appear successful in the aggregate while performing poorly within individual segments—or the reverse.

> **Key takeaway:** Always compare both the overall result and the results within meaningful subgroups before making a business decision.

---

## Conclusion

Aggregated metrics can be useful, but they can also conceal important differences in the underlying data.

Whenever a result seems surprising, break it down by relevant segments and check whether changes in group size or composition are influencing the conclusion.
